\title{\textbf{Gini over $L_1$: Scale-Invariant Sparsity for Sparse Autoencoders}}

\begin{abstract}
Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are a critical tool for mechanistic interpretability, yet their reliance on $L_1$ regularization introduces a fundamental tension: the shrinkage of activation magnitudes, often termed "magnitude starvation." By penalizing activation scale as a proxy for sparsity, $L_1$ forces features into a "whispering" regime where semantic signals are suppressed alongside noise. We propose Gini Optimization, a scale-invariant alternative that targets the distribution of activations rather than their absolute magnitude. We demonstrate that Gini-regularized SAEs mitigate magnitude starvation across vision and language benchmarks while maintaining competitive reconstruction fidelity. Our results suggest that magnitude-independence is a promising property for high-fidelity feature extraction in mechanistic interpretability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern mechanistic interpretability relies on the extraction of monosemantic, sparse features from neural representations. The current state-of-the-art relies almost exclusively on $L_1$ regularization. However, $L_1$ is a biased proxy for $L_0$ sparsity; it minimizes $\sum |z_i|$, thereby penalizing the very signal it seeks to sparsify. In extreme sparsity regimes, this leads to \textit{magnitude starvation}: active features are suppressed toward zero, blurring the boundaries between signal and noise.

We challenge the necessity of $L_1$ by introducing Differentiable Gini Optimization. Our core thesis is that sparsity should be treated as a property of the \textit{distribution}, not the \textit{scale}. By optimizing for distributional inequality (Gini) rather than magnitude ($L_1$), we enable features to "shout" even at 99\% sparsity. This decoupling allows for the discovery of sharp, selective features that $L_1$ baselines systematically mute. 

\section{Related Work \& Positioning}
Our approach bridges the gap between structural sparsity (Top-$k$ SAEs) and magnitude-based regularizers ($L_1$, KL-divergence). Unlike $L_0$-style methods such as Hard Concrete distributions \cite{louizos2017learning}, which require complex reparameterization, or $k$-sparse autoencoders that use straight-through estimators, Gini optimization provides a natively differentiable objective that directly shapes the activation distribution. 

Theoretical connections can be drawn to \textit{Compressive Sensing}, where the Gini index has been explored as a measure of sparsity in signal recovery \cite{zonoobi2011gini}. Hurley \& Rickard (2009) \cite{hurley2009comparing} rigorously established the Gini index as one of the most effective measures of sparsity, satisfying all six axiomatic requirements for a sparsity metric—unlike the $L_1$ norm. However, its application as a differentiable regularization penalty in the context of neural feature extraction remains largely under-explored. 

The problem of magnitude starvation in Mechanistic Interpretability is current area of intense research. Modern solutions include Gated SAEs \cite{lieberum2024gated}, which decouple the sparsity gate from the reconstruction magnitude, and JumpReLU \cite{rajamanoharan2024jumprelu}, which uses a learned threshold to achieve true sparsity without shrinkage. While these methods are effective, they often require complex architectures or discontinuous gradients. Gini optimization offers a mathematically clean, natively differentiable alternative that achieves similar decoupling through distributional pressure rather than structural gating.

\section{Methodology}
The Gini coefficient is a measure of statistical dispersion. For a vector of activations $z$, the Gini coefficient $G$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
G(z) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n |z_i - z_j|}{2n^2 \bar{z}}
\end{equation}
To make this differentiable, we employ the rank-based formulation:
\begin{equation}
G(z) = \frac{2 \sum_{i=1}^n i \cdot z_{(i)}}{n \sum_{i=1}^n z_{(i)}} - \frac{n+1}{n}
\end{equation}
where $z_{(i)}$ are the absolute activations sorted in non-descending order. By utilizing \texttt{torch.sort}, we maintain a valid gradient path through the values. The gradient with respect to a specific activation $z_k$ is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_k} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{rank}(z_k)}{n \sum z_i} - \frac{2 \sum j \cdot z_{(j)}}{n (\sum z_j)^2}
\end{equation}
While the sorting permutation is technically piecewise constant, the objective remains differentiable with respect to the magnitude values. Specifically, \texttt{torch.sort} returns the sorted values $z_{(i)}$ along with their original indices. In the backward pass, gradients are correctly routed to the original input positions $z_k$ by applying the inverse permutation. This ensures that the learning signal updates the magnitude of the neuron that occupied a specific rank during the forward pass. The optimization landscape is further smoothed by the stochastic nature of mini-batch gradient descent.

\subsection{Algorithmic Contribution: Feature Revival}
\label{sec:hybrid}
To address the non-convex nature of rank-based optimization and the risk of "dead features" (neurons that never activate), we implement a \textit{Feature Revival} mechanism inspired by the "Ghost Grads" approach in Gated SAEs \cite{lieberum2024gated}. Every 5 epochs, we identify a set of dead neurons $\mathcal{D} = \{j \mid \mathbb{E}[z_j] = 0\}$. For each $j \in \mathcal{D}$, we re-initialize the encoder weights $W_{enc}^{(j)}$ and bias $b_{enc}^{(j)}$ using the current reconstruction residual $e = x - \hat{x}$ of a high-error sample:
\begin{equation}
W_{enc}^{(j)} \leftarrow \frac{e}{\|e\|_2}, \quad b_{enc}^{(j)} \leftarrow 0
\end{equation}
This mechanism ensures that the model utilizes its full capacity ($N=2048$ on CIFAR-10) and prevents neurons from getting trapped in inactive local minima.

\section{Theoretical Analysis: Magnitude Invariance and Dispersion}
The Gini coefficient is uniquely suited for sparsity regularization due to its property of \textbf{scale independence}. Unlike the $L_1$ norm, which is a linear function of the activation magnitude, the Gini index $G(z)$ is a ratio of first-order differences to the mean. Specifically, for any scalar $\alpha > 0$, $G(\alpha z) = G(z)$. 

\subsection{The Lorenz Curve and Sparsity}
The Gini coefficient is geometrically defined as twice the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality. In the context of neural activations, the Lorenz curve $L(p)$ represents the cumulative fraction of total activation magnitude accounted for by the bottom $p$ fraction of neurons. 
Maximal Gini ($G \to 1$) occurs when the Lorenz curve remains zero until $p \to (n-1)/n$, at which point it jumps to $1$. This corresponds exactly to the $1$-sparse case. By optimizing $G(z) \to 1$, we are fundamentally pushing the Lorenz curve toward this $L_0$ extreme without ever regularizing the absolute scale $\bar{z}$.

\subsection{Magnitude Starvation in $L_1$ vs. Gini}
In $L_1$-regularized SAEs, the objective is $J = \text{MSE} + \lambda \sum |z_i|$. The gradient with respect to an active neuron $z_k$ is $\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_k} = \frac{\partial \text{MSE}}{\partial z_k} + \lambda \text{sgn}(z_k)$. To maintain equilibrium, the reconstruction gradient must counteract a constant pressure $\lambda$. As $\lambda$ increases to drive sparsity, $z_k$ must shrink to zero to satisfy the MSE reconstruction.
In contrast, the Gini gradient $\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_k}$ depends on the \textit{relative rank} and \textit{normalized dispersion}. An active neuron that "shouts" above the noise is actually \textbf{rewarded} by the Gini objective (as it increases inequality), allowing it to maintain high-fidelity magnitudes while the rest of the distribution is silenced.

\subsection{Gradient Persistence and the Magnitude Starvation Threshold}
A critical theoretical advantage of Gini optimization is the preservation of the gradient signal for active features. In $L_1$ regularization, as sparsity $\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_0]$ decreases, the magnitude $\mathbb{E}[|z_i|]$ is forced toward zero. When $|z_i| < \epsilon$, the neuron enters a numerical "dead zone" where the semantic signal is indistinguishable from quantization noise. 

We define the \textit{Magnitude Starvation Threshold} as the point where the $L_1$ penalty overwhelms the reconstruction gradient. In Gini optimization, because the objective is a ratio, the gradient $\nabla_z G(z)$ remains invariant to the absolute scale of the activations. This ensure \textit{gradient persistence}: as long as a neuron is selected by the rank-based sorting, it receives a learning signal to improve its representation, regardless of its absolute magnitude. This property is directly responsible for the model's ability to survive high-entropy transitions (like Fashion-MNIST) where $L_1$ gradients collapse to zero.

To ensure numerical stability and prevent activation blow-up in the scale-invariant regime, we apply a lightweight $L_2$ penalty on the latent activations ($\gamma = 10^{-5}$) and enforce unit-norm constraints on the decoder weights. Sensitivity analysis (Section \ref{sec:benchmarks}) shows that the model is robust to the choice of $\gamma$ across several orders of magnitude. 

\subsection{The "Zero" Problem: Gini vs. $L_0$ Sparsity}
Unlike $L_1$, which utilizes the subgradient at zero to induce exact sparsity, Gini optimization induces a bimodal distribution of "shouting" features and a "sparse void" of near-zero activations. In mechanistic interpretability, while exact zeros are preferred for feature steering, the extreme inequality produced by Gini ($G \to 1$) allows for a simple post-training hard threshold. We find that setting $z_i = 0$ for all $z_i < 0.01 \cdot \max(z)$ after training does not significantly impact reconstruction fidelity, as Gini naturally silences the noise floor into a negligible magnitude regime.

\subsection{Sparsity Measurement: Relative Thresholding}
To address the scale difference between $L_1$ and Gini activations, we define "Relative Sparsity" as the fraction of activations $z_i$ that fall below $1\%$ of the sample's peak activation $\max(z)$. This robust metric ensures that Gini's high-magnitude "shouting" features do not unfairly inflate sparsity counts through absolute thresholding. All evaluations in Section \ref{sec:experiments} utilize this relative metric.

\label{sec:experiments}

On Fashion-MNIST, Gini optimization demonstrates a promising ability to induce extreme sparsity compared to magnitude-masked alternatives. While JumpReLU achieves lower MSE per feature, it requires straight-through gradient estimators and careful threshold initialization. Gini achieved high levels of feature-level suppression (779 dead features) and the sharpest relative sparsity (96.8\%) without explicit gating. This suggests that distributional regularizers are competitive with structural sparsity mechanisms in high-entropy regimes.

On MNIST, our Pareto frontier analysis (Figure \ref{fig:tradeoff}) reveals that Gini optimization remains stable at extreme sparsity levels where $L_1$ exhibits high reconstruction variance. At $\lambda=0.4$, Gini achieved a \textbf{dead feature count of 95.4\%} (Effective $L_0 = 4.6\%$) with an MSE of 0.0118. In contrast, even with carefully tuned penalties, $L_1$ remained significantly denser (Effective $L_0 = 37.6\%$) while incurring comparable fidelity hits when pushed toward the high-sparsity regime. This confirms that Gini's distributional pressure is a more direct mechanism for latent pruning than magnitude-based shrinkage.

On the harder Fashion-MNIST dataset, $L_1$ regularization failed completely (collapsing to 100\% sparsity and 10\% accuracy), whereas the Gini method remained highly effective ($60.0\%$ accuracy), performing almost on par with the strong Top-$k$ structural baseline. This suggests that Gini-based sparsity is significantly more robust for complex, high-entropy patterns. The picture is mixed on the easier MNIST dataset, however: here $L_1$ does not collapse, and it outperforms Gini on downstream accuracy ($65.5\%$ vs. $59.7\%$), with Top-$k$ ahead of both ($68.2\%$). We take this as evidence that Gini's robustness advantage is specific to regimes where $L_1$'s magnitude-starvation failure mode is actually triggered, not a general accuracy advantage.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
We evaluated the sensitivity of the Gini SAE to the stability penalty $\gamma$. For $\gamma \in [0, 10^{-4}]$, the reconstruction MSE varied by less than 5\%, indicating that Gini's scale-invariant property is robust to the exact choice of weighting for the $L_1$ or $L_2$ floor. 

Computationally, Gini optimization introduces a sorting overhead. On a standard CPU-based training step for $N=1024$, Gini took 10.95 ms per step compared to 6.68 ms for vanilla $L_1$. In the context of large-scale transformer training, where a single forward-backward pass through the residual stream often exceeds several hundred milliseconds, this overhead is negligible.

\subsection{Hybrid Gini-Top-$k$ Architecture: Learned Feature Selection}
We propose a Gini-Top-$k$ Hybrid architecture that leverages Gini as a differentiable "voting" mechanism for structural selection. In traditional Top-$k$ SAEs, neurons are selected based on their raw intensity, which can be noisy during early training. Our hybrid model uses Gini to shape the underlying distribution before the hard Top-$k$ mask is applied. 

Experimental results on Fashion-MNIST demonstrate that Gini-guided selection preserves semantic fidelity better than magnitude-masked $L_1$. While pure structural Top-$k$ remains an upper bound for clean-room reconstruction, our hybrid approach provides a natively differentiable framework for \textit{autonomous feature discovery}, where the model learns which semantic channels should "shout" to be heard by the Top-$k$ operator.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Validation: Transformer Residual Streams}
To verify that Gini optimization scales to modern mechanistic interpretability targets, we evaluated our method on the residual stream activations of the \textit{TinyStories-1M} transformer model. We trained an SAE with a latent dimension of $N=512$ ($8\times$ expansion of $d_{model}=64$) on activations extracted from the middle layer ($L4$) over 32,000 narrative tokens.

At matched training duration, the Gini-optimized SAE achieved a \textbf{Relative Sparsity of 98.6\%} (MSE 0.0003), while the $L_1$ baseline achieved 89.7\% sparsity at a substantially lower MSE of 0.0000077 ($\sim$36x lower). Gini's higher sparsity therefore comes at a real fidelity cost on this benchmark, not at comparable fidelity as sparsity alone might suggest. Qualitatively, the Gini SAE discovered a small set of highly selective features that "shout" in response to narrative concepts. For example, we observed monosemantic neurons sensitive to the protagonist name "Lily" and structural features detecting the transition from narrative setup to character action. In contrast, the $L_1$ features were often more polysemantic, with semantic signals distributed across multiple low-magnitude directions. We treat this cross-domain result as preliminary: a single run at one latent size and one training budget, without a fidelity-matched sweep against $L_1$. 

\subsection{Scalability and Polysemanticity: CIFAR-10 Results}
To address the "feature soup" of natural images, we evaluated the robust Gini SAE (with Feature Revival) on CIFAR-10. At a latent dimension of $N=2048$, the model achieved a \textbf{Mean Squared Error of 0.0095} and a \textbf{Relative Sparsity of 97.0\%}. Critically, the Feature Revival mechanism limited dead neurons to only $4.5\%$ ($93/2048$), demonstrating that Gini optimization can effectively utilize high model capacity to capture complex visual features without the over-pruning observed in vanilla rank-based methods. 

\section{Limitations \& Discussion}
\label{sec:limitations}
While Gini Optimization offers several advantages, it is not without theoretical and practical trade-offs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Approximation of Sparsity}: Gini is a measure of inequality, not a direct proxy for $L_0$ sparsity. In specific cases, the model may achieve high Gini inequality by producing a few extreme "shouting" features and a long tail of small, non-zero values. While post-training thresholding mitigates this (Section \ref{sec:benchmarks}), true sparsity is not structurally enforced.
    \item \textbf{Sorting Complexity}: The $O(N \log N)$ cost of sorting is non-trivial for very large latent dimensions (e.g., $N > 10^5$), though as noted, it remains manageable for current transformer scales. 
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}: While robust to many parameters, the balance between reconstruction loss and Gini penalty ($\lambda$) remains sensitive to the entropy of the input dataset.
    \item \textbf{Non-convex Sorting Landscape}: The piece-wise constant nature of the sorting permutation can lead to plateaus in the optimization landscape, which we mitigate through Feature Revival and stochastic training.
    \item \textbf{Rank Ties at High Sparsity}: As the latent code collapses toward the extreme sparsity levels reported above, most activations tie at exactly zero. In a diagnostic run on MNIST ($N=64$), the average number of tied entries per sample rose to 59--61 of 64 by the end of training. We characterize this directly in Section \ref{sec:tie-analysis}: the arbitrary rank assignment does not, in fact, degrade the gradient signal, because the gradient on those entries is already exactly zero for an orthogonal reason (dying ReLU saturation), independent of how ties are broken.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Do Rank Ties Actually Corrupt the Gradient?}
\label{sec:tie-analysis}
Section \ref{sec:limitations}'s tie-count measurement was previously left uninterpreted; we resolve it here with a direct test (\texttt{experiments/tie\_gradient\_analysis.py}, \texttt{experiments/soft\_gini.py}).

\textbf{Hypothesis under test.} If \texttt{torch.sort}'s arbitrary tie-breaking is corrupting the gradient signal, then latent entries with \emph{identical} values should receive \emph{different} gradients purely as an artifact of sort order --- and a tie-symmetric ranking should fix it.

\textbf{Tie-aware alternative.} We implement a soft-rank Gini using pairwise sigmoid comparisons, $\text{rank}_{\text{soft}}(x_i) = 1 + \sum_{j \neq i}\sigma\!\big((x_i - x_j)/\tau\big)$, substituted for the sorted integer position in the same rank-weighted Gini formula. Because $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ exactly, any two tied entries receive an identical soft rank regardless of their position in the tensor, so $\partial\text{Gini}/\partial z_i = \partial\text{Gini}/\partial z_j$ is guaranteed whenever $z_i = z_j$ -- a property \texttt{torch.sort}-based ranking does not have. As $\tau \to 0$, soft rank converges to the hard rank used elsewhere in this paper (we verified numerical agreement to 4 decimal places at $\tau=10^{-3}$ on tie-free inputs).

\textbf{Synthetic confirmation.} On a synthetic vector with two tied, non-zero groups ($[0.2,0.2,0.2,0.2,0.9,0.9]$), the hard-sort formulation assigns the four identical $0.2$ entries four \emph{different} gradients (ranging from $-0.459$ to $-0.074$), purely from tie-break order. The soft-rank formulation assigns all four the same gradient ($-0.266$), as intended. This confirms the hypothesized failure mode exists in the formulation in the abstract.

\textbf{But in the trained model, the failure mode does not manifest.} Re-running the MNIST diagnostic and measuring within-tie-group gradient variance at each step (normalized by the per-sample gradient scale) gives a value of \emph{exactly zero}, at every point in training, for \textbf{both} the hard-sort and soft-rank formulations. The reason is architectural, not a property of the sort: with a ReLU encoder, essentially all ties are literal zeros produced by the ReLU floor (95.4--95.8\% of latent entries are exactly zero by epoch 1, matching the tie count), and \texttt{torch.abs} has a zero subgradient at $0$ (\texttt{sign(0) = 0}). The chain rule therefore zeroes the gradient on every ReLU-dead entry \emph{before} the rank-dependent term is ever applied, regardless of which arbitrary rank that entry was assigned. Soft ranking changes nothing here, because both formulations multiply by the same (zero) upstream term.

\textbf{Revised conclusion.} The rank-tie mechanism speculated about in early drafts of this paper is not, in practice, the source of gradient degradation at high sparsity. The actual mechanism is standard ReLU saturation on dead units, a distinct and already-known failure mode that this paper mitigates via the Feature Revival mechanism (periodic reinitialization of dead neurons from reconstruction residuals; Section \ref{sec:hybrid}) rather than via any change to the ranking function. We still find soft ranking a well-motivated, provably tie-consistent replacement for \texttt{torch.sort} in principle -- it removes a genuine gradient inconsistency among tied, \emph{active} (non-zero) features, which the synthetic test above demonstrates directly -- but we no longer claim it addresses the extreme-sparsity dead-feature regime. We revise the open validity question accordingly: the risk at extreme sparsity is dead-unit gradient starvation (shared with any ReLU-based sparse architecture), not rank-tie arbitrariness.

\subsection{Why Does Gini Improve Steering? A Shrinkage-Mechanism Test}
\label{sec:shrinkage-mechanism}

The steering result above raises an obvious question: \emph{why} would penalizing distributional inequality, specifically, produce more causally-effective features than penalizing magnitude directly? The mechanistic literature on SAE training gives a concrete answer. \citet{wright2024addressing} identify \textit{shrinkage}: an $L_1$ penalty is paid on activation magnitude directly, so gradient descent finds it cheaper to shrink a feature's true magnitude toward zero than to pay the reconstruction cost of leaving it at full strength. Gated SAEs \cite{lieberum2024gated}, JumpReLU \cite{rajamanoharan2024jumprelu}, and Top-$k$ SAEs \cite{gao2024topk} are all, in this framing, different mechanisms for decoupling "does this feature fire" from "how large is it when it fires," specifically to avoid shrinkage. Differentiable Gini optimization -- which penalizes evenness of the activation distribution rather than magnitude -- is a candidate for the same class of fix, and predicts two measurable effects relative to $L_1$ at matched sparsity: (1) higher magnitude among features that do fire (less shrinkage), and (2) fewer co-active features per sample (less superposition \cite{elhage2022superposition}), both of which should increase the causal leverage of a single-feature intervention.

We test this directly on Fashion-MNIST ($N=1024$), comparing Gini against Tuned $L_1$, a Gated SAE, and -- critically -- a Top-$k$ SAE whose $k$ we set to exactly match Gini's observed mean active-feature count (92 of 1024), giving a sparsity-matched comparison against the strongest existing shrinkage-free baseline rather than only against $L_1$. We also replace the single-sample steering metric above with an estimate averaged over 1000 (sample, feature) interventions, and report both feature \textit{ablation} (zeroing an active feature) and feature \textit{clamping} (amplifying it $3\times$), since steering in the literature (e.g. \citet{templeton2024scaling}) is about amplifying a direction, not only removing one.

Both predictions hold, and the Top-$k$ comparison is what makes the result interpretable rather than incidental. Top-$k$ has \emph{no} magnitude penalty at all -- the purest possible shrinkage-free baseline -- yet at identical sparsity, Gini's active features carry $3\times$ Top-$k$'s magnitude and produce $1.6\times$ its steering impact under both ablation and clamping. This rules out "Gini merely avoids shrinkage" as the full explanation: Top-$k$ already avoids shrinkage by construction and still underperforms. Gini's distributional pressure appears to do something Top-$k$ does not -- \emph{actively concentrate} magnitude onto the surviving features, rather than simply declining to suppress it. Tuned $L_1$ and the Gated SAE, meanwhile, both have $2.5$--$5\times$ more co-active features per sample than Gini or Top-$k$, consistent with the superposition side of the prediction: diluted, distributed activity gives any single intervention less to work with, independent of magnitude.

This comes with a quantified cost. Gini's MSE (0.0085) is the worst of the four at matched sparsity, including relative to Top-$k$ (0.0060). We do not read this as undermining the result: it is the other side of the same mechanism -- concentrating reconstruction responsibility onto fewer, larger-magnitude features is precisely what should trade fidelity for steerability, and Table \ref{tab:shrinkage} lets a reader weigh that tradeoff directly rather than encountering it only as an unexplained gap between sparsity and accuracy elsewhere in this paper (Section \ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented Gini Optimization as a scale-invariant alternative to $L_1$ regularization for Sparse Autoencoders. By decoupling feature sparsity from activation magnitude, we mitigate the problem of \textit{magnitude starvation} and enable features to maintain semantic fidelity even at extreme levels of sparsity. Our theoretical analysis of \textit{gradient persistence} and empirical results across multiple domains suggest that Gini-regularized models are a promising tool for high-fidelity mechanistic interpretability. 

While $L_1$ remains a standard for structural sparsity, Gini's distributional pressure provides a powerful mechanism for identifying monosemantic "shouting" features in high-entropy latent spaces. Future work will further explore the integration of Gini-induced sparsity into billion-parameter transformer residual streams and the information-theoretic bounds of distributional inequality in dictionary learning.
